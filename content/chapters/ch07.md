# Sectiō VII · The third declension: trust the genitive

**The one idea to take from this section:** in the 3rd declension, the nominative won't tell you much. The genitive will: it always ends in -is, and taking that -is away gives you the base that every other form is built on.

Sections 2–4 covered two declensions, one mostly feminine and one masculine and neuter. The 3rd declension (Latin has five in all) is bigger than either, holds all three genders, and its nominatives come in many shapes. The method, though, is the one you already use: base plus endings.

## Finding the base

Look at how far the nominative can drift from the base:

| Entry | Base | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *rēx, rēgis*, m. | rēg- | king |
| *nōmen, nōminis*, n. | nōmin- | name |
| *tempus, temporis*, n. | tempor- | time |
| *uxor, uxōris*, f. | uxōr- | wife |
| *pāx, pācis*, f. | pāc- | peace |

Nothing in *rēx* says rēg-, and nothing in *tempus* says tempor-. (*Rēx* is really rēg- plus an -s ending: g + s is written x.) That's why **the full entry matters more than ever**: nominative, genitive, and gender, every time. English derivatives can jog your memory of the base: "regal," "nominate," "temporary."

## The endings

Here are three nouns, one of each gender, with the endings alongside. A noun like *virgō, virginis*, f., whose base looks different from its nominative, takes the same endings as *cīvitās*: *virginem*, *virgine*, *virginēs*.

| Case | *amor*, m. | *cīvitās*, f. | *nōmen*, n. | m./f. ending | n. ending |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | amor | cīvitās | nōmen | (varies) | (varies) |
| gen. sg. | amōr**is** | cīvitāt**is** | nōmin**is** | -is | -is |
| dat. sg. | amōr**ī** | cīvitāt**ī** | nōmin**ī** | -ī | -ī |
| acc. sg. | amōr**em** | cīvitāt**em** | nōmen | -em | = nom. |
| abl. sg. | amōr**e** | cīvitāt**e** | nōmin**e** | -e | -e |
| nom. pl. | amōr**ēs** | cīvitāt**ēs** | nōmin**a** | -ēs | -a |
| gen. pl. | amōr**um** | cīvitāt**um** | nōmin**um** | -um | -um |
| dat. pl. | amōr**ibus** | cīvitāt**ibus** | nōmin**ibus** | -ibus | -ibus |
| acc. pl. | amōr**ēs** | cīvitāt**ēs** | nōmin**a** | -ēs | -a |
| abl. pl. | amōr**ibus** | cīvitāt**ibus** | nōmin**ibus** | -ibus | -ibus |

Wheelock's models are *rēx*, *virtūs*, *homō*, and *corpus*; the endings are the same.

Three things to notice:

- **Masculine and feminine share one set of endings.** The endings don't show gender; the dictionary entry (helped by the patterns below) does.
- **The neuter rule from section 4 still holds.** The accusative is the same as the nominative, in the singular (*nōmen*) and in the plural (*nōmina*), and the neuter plural ends in -a.
- **There's no vocative row.** Outside the 2nd declension's -us and -ius nouns, the vocative is always the same as the nominative, so from now on tables leave it out.

## Gender: some reliable patterns

Nouns for people usually take the gender of the person: *rēx* is masculine, *uxor* and *virgō* feminine. Beyond that, the endings below are nearly always reliable.

**Masculine**
- -or, -ōris: *amor*, *labor*. Many of these, in -tor, are **agent nouns**, naming the person who does something; English keeps them unchanged in "actor," "victor," and "inventor." The nominative has a short o, because a long vowel shortens before a final -r: *amor*, but *amōris*.

**Feminine** (many of these are **abstract nouns**, names for qualities and ideas)
- -tās, -tātis: *cīvitās*. English turns this into "-ty" (*cīvitās* itself became English "city" by way of later Latin).
- -tūs, -tūtis: *virtūs*.
- -tūdō, -tūdinis: the source of English "-tude," as in "magnitude."
- -tiō, -tiōnis: the source of English "-tion."

**Neuter**
- -us, with base in -or- or -er-: *tempus, temporis*; *corpus, corporis*. Here the o of the base is short, unlike the long ō of masculine *amōris*.
- -e, -al, -ar: the shapes behind English "animal" and "exemplar."
- -men, -minis: *carmen*, *nōmen*.

*Uxor* is the useful exception: it ends in -or, but a wife is female, so it is feminine.

## Adjectives with 3rd-declension nouns

A 3rd-declension noun often takes a 1st/2nd-declension adjective. They agree in gender, number, and case, but they keep their own endings, just as *poēta Rōmānus* did in section 3.

| Case | "a foolish king," sg. | pl. | "an ancient name," sg. | pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. | rēx stult**us** | rēg**ēs** stult**ī** | nōmen antīqu**um** | nōmin**a** antīqu**a** |
| gen. | rēg**is** stult**ī** | rēg**um** stult**ōrum** | nōmin**is** antīqu**ī** | nōmin**um** antīqu**ōrum** |
| dat. | rēg**ī** stult**ō** | rēg**ibus** stult**īs** | nōmin**ī** antīqu**ō** | nōmin**ibus** antīqu**īs** |
| acc. | rēg**em** stult**um** | rēg**ēs** stult**ōs** | nōmen antīqu**um** | nōmin**a** antīqu**a** |
| abl. | rēg**e** stult**ō** | rēg**ibus** stult**īs** | nōmin**e** antīqu**ō** | nōmin**ibus** antīqu**īs** |

Matching words don't need matching letters. *Cīvitās lībera*, "a free state," becomes *cīvitātis līberae* in the genitive.

## Notes from the word list

- **Check the genitive before you decide the declension.** *Corpus* and *tempus* end in -us, but their genitives, *corporis* and *temporis*, make them 3rd-declension neuters, not 2nd-declension masculines. Going the other way, **rēgīna**, **littera**, and **terra** are ordinary 1st-declension nouns, even though *rēgīna* belongs in meaning with *rēx*.
- Two nouns change meaning in the plural, as *animus* did: **mōs**, "custom," but *mōrēs*, "character, morals"; **littera**, "a letter of the alphabet," but *litterae*, "a letter (that you send)" or "literature." *Mōrēs cīvitātis nōn sunt bonī.* "The state's morals are not good."
- **Homō** is "human being," man or woman; **vir** is "man" as opposed to woman, often with a sense of "hero."
- **Labor** has a short o in the nominative but a long ō in *labōris* (the a is short in both).
- **Audeō**, "dare," takes a complementary infinitive: *Audēsne ibi manēre?* "Do you dare to stay there?" Its third principal part, *ausus sum*, looks odd; section 34 explains why.
- **Sub**, "under," takes the ablative when something is at rest (*sub terrā*, "under the ground") and the accusative when something moves under or toward something. As a prefix it often changes its b to match the next consonant: "succeed," "suffix," "support."
- **Post** takes the accusative and points backward, in time ("after") or in place ("behind"): *Post labōrem cēnābimus.* "After work we will dine."
- **Necō** is "kill": *Tyrannus multōs hominēs necābat.* "The tyrant was killing many people."

## Some practice sentences

- *Rēx et rēgīna pācem amant.* "The king and queen love peace."
- *Uxor rēgis carmina amābat.* "The king's wife used to love songs."
- *Nōmen tuum in litterīs vidēbam.* "I kept seeing your name in the letter."
- *Corpora sāna habēmus.* "We have healthy bodies."
- *Virtūs virginis erat magna.* "The maiden's courage was great."

## A reading tip: endings that look familiar

Some 3rd-declension endings are spelled like endings you already know, but mean something else.

- **-ī**: *rēgī* is dative singular (3rd), but *amīcī* is genitive singular or nominative plural (2nd).
- **-us**: *amīcus* is a masculine nominative (2nd), but *corpus* is a neuter nominative or accusative (3rd).
- **-um**: *rēgum* is genitive plural (3rd), but *dōnum* is a nominative or accusative singular (2nd).
- **-e**: *rēge* is ablative (3rd), but *amīce* is vocative (2nd).

The cure is knowing each noun's entry. After that, word order and context settle most cases.

## Check yourself

1. What is the base of *virtūs, virtūtis*, and what is its accusative singular?
   - Hint: drop the -is from the genitive.
   - Answer: *virtūt-*; accusative *virtūtem*.
2. Is *corpora* singular or plural, and which cases can it be?
   - Hint: it's a neuter.
   - Answer: plural, nominative or accusative (neuter -a).
3. Translate *Hominēs pācem laudant sed bellum amant.*
   - Hint: *hominēs* comes from *homō, hominis*.
   - Answer: "People praise peace but love war."
4. Why is *uxor* feminine, when most -or nouns are masculine?
   - Hint: think about who the word names.
   - Answer: nouns for people follow the person's sex, and a wife is female.
5. Give the dative singular of *tempus malum*.
   - Hint: each word keeps its own declension.
   - Answer: *temporī malō*.
