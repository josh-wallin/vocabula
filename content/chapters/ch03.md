# Sectiō III · A second set of endings, mostly masculine

**The one idea to take from this section:** the 2nd declension is a new family of nouns with its own endings, and most of its nouns in -us are masculine. *Populus*, "a people," is to this family what *rosa* was to the 1st.

The method is unchanged. Find the base from the genitive, then attach endings. Only the endings are new, and several of them will look familiar.

## What the 2nd declension holds

Nouns of the 2nd declension are regularly masculine or neuter; a handful of feminines turn up in later sections. This section covers the masculines; neuters wait for section 4.

Masculine nouns here come in two shapes:

- **-us** in the nominative singular, by far the most common: *populus*, *numerus*, *fīlius*;
- **-er** (or, in one word, **-ir**): *puer*, *ager*, *vir*.

## Masculines in -us

Here is *populus antīquus*, "an ancient people," with the adjective following the noun exactly.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | popul**us** antīqu**us** | popul**ī** antīqu**ī** |
| genitive | popul**ī** antīqu**ī** | popul**ōrum** antīqu**ōrum** |
| dative | popul**ō** antīqu**ō** | popul**īs** antīqu**īs** |
| accusative | popul**um** antīqu**um** | popul**ōs** antīqu**ōs** |
| ablative | popul**ō** antīqu**ō** | popul**īs** antīqu**īs** |
| vocative | popul**e** antīqu**e** | popul**ī** antīqu**ī** |

Wheelock's models are *amīcus* and *magnus*; the endings are identical.

### Old endings in new clothes

Set this beside the 1st declension and the family resemblance shows:

| | 1st declension | 2nd declension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| acc. sg. | -am | -um |
| gen. pl. | -ārum | -ōrum |
| acc. pl. | -ās | -ōs |
| dat./abl. pl. | -īs | -īs |

The dative and ablative plural are exactly the same in both families. The others swap a long ā for a long ō, or a for u.

### Forms that could be several cases

As in section 2, some endings do double or triple duty:

- *populī*: genitive singular, nominative plural, or vocative plural;
- *populō*: dative or ablative singular;
- *populīs*: dative or ablative plural.

The verb's number, the sense, and the other words decide. *Amīcī fīliam vident* has a plural verb, so *amīcī* is most naturally its subject (a genitive would normally follow *fīliam*): "The friends see the daughter." In *Fīliam amīcī videt* the verb is singular, so *amīcī* can't be its subject and is best read as a genitive: "He sees his friend's daughter."

## Masculines in -er and -ir

These nouns end in -er (or -ir) in the nominative singular, and the nominative doesn't always show the base. In *puer* the nominative and the base are the same; in *ager* they aren't, since the base is *agr-*. That's why the genitive matters:

- *puer, puerī*, boy: the e stays, so the base is *puer-*;
- *ager, agrī*, field: the e drops out, so the base is *agr-*. Most -er nouns behave this way;
- *vir, virī*, man: the only noun in -ir. Its base is *vir-*.

This is why every 2nd-declension noun is learned with its genitive. English derivatives can jog the memory: "puerile" keeps the e, "agriculture" drops it, and "virile" keeps the base *vir-*.

| Case | boy | field | man | endings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | puer | ager | vir | -er (or -ir) |
| gen. sg. | puer**ī** | agr**ī** | vir**ī** | -ī |
| dat. sg. | puer**ō** | agr**ō** | vir**ō** | -ō |
| acc. sg. | puer**um** | agr**um** | vir**um** | -um |
| abl. sg. | puer**ō** | agr**ō** | vir**ō** | -ō |
| voc. sg. | puer | ager | vir | -er (or -ir) |
| nom. pl. | puer**ī** | agr**ī** | vir**ī** | -ī |
| gen. pl. | puer**ōrum** | agr**ōrum** | vir**ōrum** | -ōrum |
| dat. pl. | puer**īs** | agr**īs** | vir**īs** | -īs |
| acc. pl. | puer**ōs** | agr**ōs** | vir**ōs** | -ōs |
| abl. pl. | puer**īs** | agr**īs** | vir**īs** | -īs |
| voc. pl. | puer**ī** | agr**ī** | vir**ī** | -ī |

Wheelock declines *puer* and *ager*; *vir* is added here because it is the one -ir noun.

After the nominative, the endings are the same as for *populus*. The vocative singular of an -er noun stays the same as its nominative.

## Agreement, again

An adjective agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case. Section 2 warned that this does not mean matching endings, and the 2nd declension shows why:

- *ager antīquus*, an ancient field: the noun ends in -er, the adjective in -us;
- *Ō puer avāre!* "Greedy boy!": vocative *puer* keeps its -er, vocative *avāre* has -e;
- *nauta avārus*, a greedy sailor: *nauta* is a 1st-declension noun, but it is masculine, so its adjective takes 2nd-declension masculine endings.

The adjective's dictionary entry now makes full sense: *avārus, avāra, avārum*. The masculine forms come from this section, the feminine forms from section 2. The neuter arrives in section 4.

## The vocative: where it finally differs

In nearly every noun so far, the vocative has looked exactly like the nominative. There is one exception: **the singular of 2nd-declension nouns and adjectives ending in -us**, which take **-e**.

- *Valē, amīce!* "Goodbye, friend!"
- *Ō popule Rōmāne!* "O Roman people!"

In the plural, vocative and nominative are always the same: *Salvēte, amīcī!*

Two smaller rules:

- nouns in **-ius**, including names, end in **-ī** in the vocative singular: *fīlius* → *fīlī*; *Cornēlius* → *Cornēlī*;
- the adjective *meus* has the vocative singular *mī*: *mī amīce*, "my friend."

## Apposition

Sometimes a noun is followed by a second noun that names the same person or thing a different way. The second is an **appositive** (Latin *ad* + *positus*, "placed next to").

*Mārcus, amīcus meus, multōs agrōs habet.* "Marcus, my friend, has many fields."

*Amīcus* explains who Marcus is, and stands beside him in the same case. An appositive:

- always matches its noun in **case**;
- usually matches it in **number**;
- often, though not always, in **gender**;
- is normally set off by commas, which makes it easy to spot.

Another: *Iūliam, fīliam agricolae, laudant.* "They praise Julia, the farmer's daughter." Both *Iūliam* and *fīliam* are accusative.

## Word order

Section 2 noted that Latin often puts the verb last. A fuller default for a plain clause:

1. subject, with its modifiers;
2. indirect object;
3. direct object;
4. adverbs or adverbial phrases;
5. the verb.

*Agricola fīliīs agrōs hodiē dat.* "Today the farmer is giving his sons fields." Adjectives (except the size and quantity words from section 2) and genitives normally follow the word they describe.

Roman writers broke this pattern constantly, for variety or for emphasis. That's possible because the endings, not the positions, carry the grammar. These all mean "The Roman poet gives roses to his friends":

- *Poēta Rōmānus amīcīs rosās dat.*
- *Amīcīs poēta Rōmānus rosās dat.*
- *Rosās amīcīs dat poēta Rōmānus.*

What changes is the emphasis. The first and last positions carry the most weight, so the third version leans on the roses and on who is giving them. *Rōmānus* modifies *poēta* wherever it sits, because only *poēta* shares its gender, number, and case.

Try the same thing in English and the meaning changes: "The friends give roses to the Roman poet" is a different event. English needs its word order because it has few endings.

## Things English adds

Latin regularly leaves out possessives when the owner is obvious. Supply them, just as you supply "a" and "the":

*Fēmina fīliābus et fīliīs rosās dat.* "The woman gives roses to her daughters and sons."

That sentence also shows an oddity in this section's word list. *Fīlia* uses a special form, *fīliābus*, for both its dative plural and its ablative plural, so it never clashes with *fīliīs*, "to the sons."

Another oddity: *paucī, paucae, pauca*, "few," means more than one by definition, so it is used almost only in the plural (the dictionary says "usually pl."). *Paucōs amīcōs habēmus.* "We have few friends."

## Two word-list reminders

- A noun's base comes from its **genitive**; an adjective's base comes most safely from its **feminine** (*avāra* → *avār-*).
- A noun has one fixed gender (a very few words can be either). An adjective has all three, so it can agree with any noun.

## A reading tip: et . . . et

When *et* appears twice, read the pair as "both . . . and." The two halves must be the same kind of thing: *Vir et agrōs et pecūniam habet.* "The man has both fields and money." The first *et* can't join *vir* to *agrōs*, since one is nominative and the other accusative.

## Check yourself

1. What is the base of *ager*, and how do you know?
   - Hint: don't trust the nominative.
   - Answer: *agr-*, from the genitive *agrī*.
2. Say "Hello, Roman farmer!" to one farmer.
   - Hint: *agricola* is 1st declension, but what gender is it?
   - Answer: *Salvē, agricola Rōmāne!* The noun keeps its 1st-declension vocative; the masculine adjective takes -e.
3. Translate *Numerus virōrum avārōrum est magnus.*
   - Hint: find the genitive plural first.
   - Answer: "The number of greedy men is large."
4. In *Cornēlium, poētam Rōmānum, semper laudāmus*, why is *poētam* accusative?
   - Hint: whose case does an appositive copy?
   - Answer: it is in apposition to *Cornēlium*, the direct object, so it matches its case. ("We always praise Cornelius, the Roman poet.")
5. Which cases can *fīliīs* be, and are they sons or daughters?
   - Hint: compare *fīliābus*.
   - Answer: dative or ablative plural of *fīlius*, so "sons." The daughters' form would be *fīliābus*.
