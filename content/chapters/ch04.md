# Sectiō IV · Things, and the verb "to be"

**The one idea to take from this section:** a neuter noun looks the same whether it is the subject or the object. *Bellum* can be "war" doing something or "war" having something done to it; only the rest of the sentence can tell you which.

This section finishes the 2nd declension with its neuter nouns, completes the adjective in all three genders, and adds *sum*, "I am," the most common verb in the language.

## Neuter nouns

The 1st declension has no neuters at all. The 2nd has a great many, and most of them name things or ideas rather than people: *bellum*, war; *ōtium*, leisure; *perīculum*, danger.

Here is *bellum parvum*, "a small war," beside *officium*, "duty," a noun whose base ends in -i-.

| Case | *bellum parvum*, sg. | *officium*, sg. | *bellum parvum*, pl. | *officium*, pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | bell**um** parv**um** | offici**um** | bell**a** parv**a** | offici**a** |
| genitive | bell**ī** parv**ī** | offici**ī** | bell**ōrum** parv**ōrum** | offici**ōrum** |
| dative | bell**ō** parv**ō** | offici**ō** | bell**īs** parv**īs** | offici**īs** |
| accusative | bell**um** parv**um** | offici**um** | bell**a** parv**a** | offici**a** |
| ablative | bell**ō** parv**ō** | offici**ō** | bell**īs** parv**īs** | offici**īs** |
| vocative | bell**um** parv**um** | offici**um** | bell**a** parv**a** | offici**a** |

Wheelock's models are *dōnum*, *cōnsilium*, and *magnum*; the pattern is identical.

### The neuter rule

Compare this table with the masculine one in section 3. The genitive, dative, and ablative are exactly the same, and so is the accusative singular, -um. Only the nominative, accusative, and vocative can differ from the masculine, and in the neuter those three cases always share one form:

**A neuter noun, in any declension, uses one form for three cases: nominative, accusative, and vocative.** In the 2nd declension that form is -um in the singular and -a in the plural.

So in *Perīculum nautās terret*, "Danger frightens the sailors," *perīculum* must be the subject: the verb is singular, and *nautās* is clearly accusative. In *Perīculum nautae vident*, "The sailors see the danger," the plural verb makes *nautae* its most natural subject, which leaves *perīculum* as the object. Vocatives rarely cause trouble, since commas set them apart.

### The trap in -a

A neuter plural ends in -a, just like a 1st-declension nominative singular. *Dōna*, a neuter, is "gifts," not "a gift"; *cūra*, a feminine, is "care," one thing, not several. The only protection is knowing each noun's gender, which is why the dictionary entry includes it. A short way to write that entry is *dōnum, -ī, n.*, and the app uses it from here on.

### Contracted genitives

Nouns whose base ends in -i- (*officium*, *cōnsilium*, *fīlius*) often shorten the genitive singular to one ī: *cōnsilī* for *cōnsiliī*, *fīlī* for *fīliī*. In Cicero and Vergil the single -ī is actually the regular form, so expect it throughout the *Aeneid*. This app writes the full -iī for now. Don't confuse genitive *fīlī*, "of the son," with vocative *fīlī*, "O son": the spelling is the same, and only the sentence tells them apart.

## The whole adjective

You have now seen every form of a regular adjective like *bonus, bona, bonum*, "good." It keeps one base, *bon-*, and borrows its endings from two declensions: 2nd for masculine and neuter, 1st for feminine.

| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | bon**us** | bon**a** | bon**um** |
| gen. sg. | bon**ī** | bon**ae** | bon**ī** |
| dat. sg. | bon**ō** | bon**ae** | bon**ō** |
| acc. sg. | bon**um** | bon**am** | bon**um** |
| abl. sg. | bon**ō** | bon**ā** | bon**ō** |
| voc. sg. | bon**e** | bon**a** | bon**um** |
| nom. pl. | bon**ī** | bon**ae** | bon**a** |
| gen. pl. | bon**ōrum** | bon**ārum** | bon**ōrum** |
| dat. pl. | bon**īs** | bon**īs** | bon**īs** |
| acc. pl. | bon**ōs** | bon**ās** | bon**a** |
| abl. pl. | bon**īs** | bon**īs** | bon**īs** |
| voc. pl. | bon**ī** | bon**ae** | bon**a** |

Wheelock uses *magnus* for this table. Adjectives of this type are listed in short form as *bonus, -a, -um*. Whatever noun the adjective describes, it takes the matching gender, number, and case.

## *Sum*, "I am"

English "to be" is irregular (*am, is, are, was*), and so is its Latin counterpart. The personal endings are the familiar ones from section 1, but the stem shifts so much that the six forms are simply memorized.

| | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | *sum*, I am | *sumus*, we are |
| 2nd person | *es*, you are | *estis*, you (pl.) are |
| 3rd person | *est*, he, she, or it is | *sunt*, they are |

The infinitive is *esse*, "to be." Notice the -m of *sum*: it is the alternative 1st-person ending promised in section 1. *Sum* can also mean "exist": *Perīcula semper sunt.* "There are always dangers" (literally, "dangers always exist").

*Est* and *sunt* can also mean "there is" and "there are," so look for that reading when the subject follows the verb: *Sunt perīcula in bellō.* "There are dangers in war."

*Sum* never acts on anything, so it has no direct object; it is an **intransitive linking verb**. Because it links its subject to a description rather than describing an action, it isn't called active or passive. Other intransitive verbs, like *errō* and *valeō*, are still active.

## Predicate nominatives

Every sentence divides into two parts:

- the **subject**, with whatever modifies it;
- the **predicate**: the verb plus everything that depends on it.

A linking verb connects the subject to a word on the predicate side that renames or describes it. That word is a **predicate noun** or **predicate adjective**; both are called **predicate nominatives**. Since it describes the subject, it matches the subject in **case** and **number**, and in **gender** whenever it can.

- *Magistra mea est fēmina Rōmāna.* "My teacher is a Roman woman."
- *Ōtium nōn semper est bonum.* "Leisure is not always good."
- *Cōnsilia tua sunt stulta.* "Your plans are foolish."
- *Estis nautae.* "You are sailors." (A predicate noun keeps its own gender: *nautae* stays masculine even if the subject is a group of women.)
- *Hūmānī esse dēbēmus.* "We ought to be kind." The infinitive *esse* links its predicate adjective to the subject of *dēbēmus*.

If the subject is two nouns of different genders, the predicate adjective is plural. For people it is usually masculine: *Agricola et fīlia sunt avārī*, "The farmer and his daughter are greedy." For things it is often neuter, whatever the nouns' genders: *Bellum et īra sunt mala.* "War and anger are bad." Sometimes it simply agrees with the nearer noun.

### Two kinds of adjective, so far

- **Attributive:** part of the noun phrase. *Amīcus vērus nōn errat.* "A true friend doesn't go astray."
- **Predicate:** stated about the subject through the verb. *Amīcus est vērus.* "The friend is true."

## Adjectives used as nouns

Latin often drops the noun and lets the adjective stand alone, the way English says "the brave" or "the unknown." This is a **substantive** adjective. Its gender and number tell you what English noun to supply:

| Form | Gender, number | Supply | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *bonī* | masc. pl. | men, people | *Bonī ōtium amant.* Good people love leisure. |
| *stultam* | fem. sg. | woman | *Stultam nōn moneō.* I don't advise the foolish woman. |
| *vēra* | neut. pl. | things | *Vēra vident.* They see the truth. |
| *multī* | masc. pl. | many men, many people | *Multī dē bellō cōgitant.* Many people are thinking about the war. |

Neuter plurals like *vēra* ("true things") and *mala* ("bad things") often sound best as one English noun: "the truth," "evils." Some substantives are common enough to get their own dictionary entry: *stultus, stultī, m.*, a fool.

## Notes from the word list

- **Nihil**, "nothing," first seen in section 1, is a neuter noun that is **indeclinable**: one form serves as subject and as object; its other cases are borrowed from other words you'll meet later. *Nihil est vērum.* "Nothing is true." *Nihil habēmus.* "We have nothing."
- **Bellum** (war, a neuter noun) and **bellus, -a, -um** (pretty) look alike. *Bella* can be "wars" or a form of "pretty" (feminine singular or neuter plural); gender and context sort them out.
- **Magister** (genitive *magistrī*), masculine, declines like *ager* (base *magistr-*), and **magistra, -ae, f.** like *rosa*.
- Many words have several meanings. Pick the one that fits: *cūrae* in a sentence about money is likely "worries," and *cōnsilium* about a war is likely "plan," not "wisdom."

## A preview: the infinitive as a noun

An infinitive can serve as the subject of a sentence. It then acts as a neuter noun with only one form: *Errāre nōn est malum.* "Making mistakes is not bad." A predicate adjective describing an infinitive is neuter singular. Later sections do more with infinitives.

## Check yourself

1. Translate *Dōna puellam iuvant.*
   - Hint: which word has to be the subject, given the verb's number?
   - Answer: "The gifts help the girl." The plural verb rules out *puellam*, which is accusative anyway.
2. Is *perīcula* singular or plural? Which cases could it be?
   - Hint: check its gender before trusting the -a.
   - Answer: neuter plural: nominative, accusative, or vocative.
3. Say "You are good boys" to a group.
   - Hint: you're speaking to more than one; the predicate matches the subject.
   - Answer: *Estis puerī bonī.*
4. Translate *Paucī ōtium habent.*
   - Hint: *paucī* is masculine plural with no noun.
   - Answer: "Few people have leisure."
5. Why is it *Remedium est bonum* but *Remedia sunt bona*?
   - Hint: what three things must a predicate adjective share with its subject?
   - Answer: the adjective matches the subject in number, gender, and case, so a neuter plural subject takes *bona*.
