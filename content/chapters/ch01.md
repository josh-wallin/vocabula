# Sectiō I · The verb carries the subject

**The one idea to take from this section:** a single Latin verb can be a whole sentence. *Laudat.* means "She praises" (or "He praises," or "It praises"). The person doing the praising is packed into the ending.

English signals who is acting mostly with separate words: *I*, *you*, *they*. Latin signals it with the ending of the verb. Once you can read a verb ending, you always know who the subject is, even when the sentence never names them.

## The words Latin uses

Latin sorts its words into the same families English does. You'll meet them in this order over the first few sections.

| Kind of word | What it does | First seen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| verb | an action or a state | this section |
| adverb | tells when, how, or how much; attaches to verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs | this section |
| noun | names a person, place, thing, or idea | section 2 |
| adjective | describes a noun | section 2 |
| preposition | links a noun to the rest of the sentence (*without* money) | section 2 |
| conjunction | joins words or clauses (*and*, *but*, *if*) | sections 1–2 |
| interjection | an exclamation (*Ō!*) | section 2 |
| pronoun | stands in for a noun (*mē*, me; *quid*, what) | a few words now; fully in section 11 |

## Five things every verb form tells you

Each Latin verb form answers five questions at once. Most of them have only one possible answer in this section, but it helps to know the full set from the start.

| Feature | Question it answers | Options | In this section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| person | Who is doing it, relative to the speaker? | 1st (I, we), 2nd (you), 3rd (he, she, it, they) | all three |
| number | One actor or more? | singular, plural | both |
| tense | When? | six in all: present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, future perfect | present only |
| mood | Is it a statement, a command, or something hypothetical? | indicative (statements and questions), imperative (commands), subjunctive (later) | indicative and imperative |
| voice | Does the subject act or get acted on? | active, passive | active only |

A verb that can take a direct object (*I see the gate*) is called **transitive**. Only transitive verbs have a full passive, which arrives in section 18.

A verb form that shows person and number is called **finite**. The **infinitive** ("to praise") is not finite: it names the action without saying who does it. Listing all of a verb's finite forms in order is called **conjugating** it.

## Step 1: find the stem

Every regular verb is built from a stem plus an ending. The infinitive tells you the stem and the verb's family, its **conjugation**.

| Infinitive | Ending | Stem vowel | Conjugation | Stem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *amāre*, to love | -āre | ā | 1st | *amā-* |
| *vidēre*, to see | -ēre | ē | 2nd | *vidē-* |

So the rule is: **take the infinitive, cut off -re, and what's left is the present stem.** Latin has four conjugations; the 3rd and 4th arrive in sections 8 and 10.

If you're doing daily conjugation worksheets, this is the key step: the infinitive's long vowel, ā or ē, settles the conjugation.

## Step 2: add a personal ending

In the active voice, six endings mark person and number. Learn them as a chant, since they appear in nearly every sentence you'll ever read.

| | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | -ō (or -m) | -mus |
| 2nd person | -s | -tis |
| 3rd person | -t | -nt |

The alternative 1st-person ending -m shows up later, in other tenses and in *sum*, "I am."

Stem plus ending gives the present tense:

| | 1st conjugation: *amō*, I love | 2nd conjugation: *videō*, I see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | *amō* | *videō* |
| you (sg.) | *amās* | *vidēs* |
| he, she, it | *amat* | *videt* |
| we | *amāmus* | *vidēmus* |
| you (pl.) | *amātis* | *vidētis* |
| they | *amant* | *vident* |

Wheelock's models are *laudō* and *moneō*; they follow exactly the same pattern (*laudās, laudat*; *monēs, monet*).

### One Latin present, three English ones

English has three ways to talk about the present, and Latin covers all of them with one form. *Vident* can be "they see," "they are seeing," or "they do see." Choose whichever sounds natural in context. "Do" works well in questions and denials: *Nōn vident*, "They don't see."

## Why some long vowels go short

Look at the table again: *amās* and *amāmus* keep the long ā, but *amat* and *amant* don't. That isn't random. A long vowel usually loses its length when it is crowded by certain sounds (there are a few exceptions you'll meet later):

1. right before another vowel: *videō*, not *\*vidēō*;
2. right before a final -m, -r, or -t: *amat*, not *\*amāt*;
3. right before nt or nd, anywhere in the word: *amant*, not *\*amānt*.

(An asterisk marks a form that doesn't exist.)

The 1st conjugation goes one step further in the "I" form: the stem vowel vanishes entirely, swallowed by the ending. Stem *amā-* plus *-ō* would give *\*amāō*; the ā is absorbed, leaving *amō*.

One verb in this section's list breaks the pattern: *dō, dare*, to give, has a short a in its stem (*damus*, *datis*, *dant*). Only *dās* and the command *dā* have a long one.

Macrons are part of the spelling, so write them every time. Accent marks are not part of normal spelling and are usually left out: stress follows fixed rules, set out in Wheelock's introduction.

## Commands

The **imperative** gives orders. It's the easiest form in the language:

For the 1st and 2nd conjugations:

- to one person: the bare stem: *Amā!* "Love!" *Vidē!* "See!"
- to more than one: stem + -te: *Amāte!* *Vidēte!*

Two everyday Roman phrases are imperatives of verbs from this section's list: *Salvē!* / *Salvēte!* ("Be well!" used as "Hello!") and *Valē!* / *Valēte!* ("Be strong!" used as "Goodbye!"). Use the -te form when you greet or leave a group.

## Adverbs

Adverbs add detail to verbs, adjectives, and other adverbs. In Latin an adverb usually sits just before the word it modifies. Two common ones are in this section's vocabulary:

- *nōn*, not: *Nōn errās.* "You aren't wrong."
- *saepe*, often: *Saepe cōgitat.* "She often thinks."

## How dictionaries list a verb

A Latin dictionary entry gives a verb in several forms, its **principal parts**, usually four of them: *amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum*. You need the first two now:

1. the "I" form of the present, *amō*;
2. the present infinitive, *amāre*, which gives you the stem and the conjugation.

The last two build the perfect tenses and the passive participle in later sections. The app holds them back until then, so you can learn each verb by its first two forms for now.

## Reading a sentence

1. **Read it aloud, for meaning.** Latin was heard before it was read.
2. **Go to the verb.** It is often the last word.
3. **Check its ending.** If it is 1st or 2nd person, the verb already names its subject ("I," "we," or "you"). If it is 3rd person (-t or -nt), the subject may be a noun elsewhere in the sentence, often near the start.
4. **Expect subject, then object, then verb.** That order (SOV) is common in Latin, where English prefers subject, verb, object.

A few sentences built only from this section's words:

- *Quid vidēs? Nihil videō.* "What do you see? I see nothing."
- *Sī errās, mē vocā.* "If you get lost, call me."
- *Saepe errāmus.* "We often make mistakes."

Notice that *sī*, "if," is a conjunction: it joins the condition to the command. Section 2 says more about conjunctions.

## Check yourself

Try each one before reading the hint.

1. Which conjugation is *terrēre*, to frighten, and what is its stem?
   - Hint: what vowel comes right before -re?
   - Answer: 2nd conjugation; stem *terrē-*.
2. Translate *Vocant.*
   - Hint: whose ending is -nt?
   - Answer: "They call" (or "they are calling," "they do call").
3. Why is it *vocat*, "she calls," with a short a, when the stem *vocā-* has a long one?
   - Hint: look at the letter that ends the word.
   - Answer: a long vowel goes short before a final -t.
4. Tell a group of friends "Think!"
   - Hint: commands to more than one person add something to the stem.
   - Answer: *Cōgitāte!*
5. Translate *Nōn valeō.*
   - Hint: *valeō* is the verb hidden inside the Roman "goodbye."
   - Answer: "I'm not well."
