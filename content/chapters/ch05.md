# Sectiō V · "Will" and "was": two more tenses

**The one idea to take from this section:** a Latin verb can carry a small **tense sign** between its stem and its ending. Slip in -bi- and the action moves into the future; slip in -ba- and it moves into the past.

So far every verb has been in the present. This section adds the future ("I will call") and the imperfect ("I was calling") for the 1st and 2nd conjugations, and finishes with a family of adjectives that end in -er.

## The present system

The present, future, and imperfect are built on the same foundation: the **present stem** you learned to find in section 1 (the infinitive minus -re: *servā-*, *habē-*). Together the three tenses are called the **present system**. Learn one stem and you can make all three.

A Latin verb in these tenses has three pieces, and English needs a separate word for each:

| Latin | stem | tense sign | ending | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *vocābit* | vocā- | -bi- | -t | he **will** call |
| *vocābat* | vocā- | -ba- | -t | he **was** calling |

A handy link: the future sign has an **i**, like "w**i**ll"; the imperfect sign has an **a**, like "w**a**s."

## The future: -bi-

Here is the future of *servō*, "to keep," and *habeō*, "to have."

| | 1st conj. | 2nd conj. | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | servā**bō** | habē**bō** | I will keep / have |
| 2nd sg. | servā**bis** | habē**bis** | you will keep / have |
| 3rd sg. | servā**bit** | habē**bit** | he, she, it will keep / have |
| 1st pl. | servā**bimus** | habē**bimus** | we will keep / have |
| 2nd pl. | servā**bitis** | habē**bitis** | you (pl.) will keep / have |
| 3rd pl. | servā**bunt** | habē**bunt** | they will keep / have |

Wheelock's models are *laudō* and *moneō*; every 1st- and 2nd-conjugation verb follows this pattern.

### bō, bi, bi, bi, bi, bu

The sign isn't -bi- everywhere. It fuses with the ending as -bō in the 1st person singular and shifts to -bu- in the 3rd plural; the four forms between keep -bi-. Say the run aloud a few times (bō, bis, bit, bimus, bitis, bunt) until it sounds automatic.

One small trap: *dō* has a short stem vowel, so its future is *dabō, dabis, dabit* and its imperfect *dabam, dabās, dabat*, with a short a throughout.

## The imperfect: -ba-

| | 1st conj. | 2nd conj. | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | servā**bam** | habē**bam** | I was keeping / having |
| 2nd sg. | servā**bās** | habē**bās** | you were keeping |
| 3rd sg. | servā**bat** | habē**bat** | he, she, it was keeping |
| 1st pl. | servā**bāmus** | habē**bāmus** | we were keeping |
| 2nd pl. | servā**bātis** | habē**bātis** | you (pl.) were keeping |
| 3rd pl. | servā**bant** | habē**bant** | they were keeping |

This -ba- sign marks the imperfect in all four conjugations, not just these two.

The imperfect uses -m, not -ō, for "I." It is the alternative 1st-person ending you met with *sum*.

### Why some a's are short

The sign is really -bā-, but its vowel is short in -bam, -bat, and -bant. That is the rule from section 1 at work: a long vowel goes short before a final -m, -r, or -t, and also, anywhere in a word, before -nt or a second vowel. The same rule explains *servat* beside *servās*.

## Translating the future

Use "will": *Crās cēnābimus.* "Tomorrow we will dine." *Fortūna tē iuvābit.* "Luck will help you." "Shall" is correct after "I" and "we," but it sounds old-fashioned to most speakers now.

## Translating the imperfect

"Imperfect" comes from a Latin word meaning "not finished." The tense pictures a past action as ongoing rather than as a single completed event. That covers several English ideas:

| Idea | *Nautās vocābāmus* can mean |
| --- | --- |
| in progress | We were calling the sailors. |
| repeated | We kept calling the sailors. |
| habitual | We used to call the sailors. |
| attempted | We tried to call the sailors. |
| beginning | We began to call the sailors. |

Context chooses. *Fēminae dē bellō cōgitābant* is most naturally "The women were thinking about the war"; *Poēta verba pulchra amābat* is more likely "The poet used to love beautiful words."

With an adverb that already implies repetition, a plain English past often reads best: *Agricola fīlium saepe culpābat.* "The farmer often blamed his son." *Semper* works the same way.

## Adjectives in -er

A few adjectives of the *bonus* type end in -er in the masculine nominative singular, just as some 2nd-declension nouns do. As with *puer* and *ager*, some keep the e in the rest of their forms and some drop it.

| Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Base | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *līber* | *lībera* | *līberum* | līber- (keeps e) | free |
| *noster* | *nostra* | *nostrum* | nostr- (drops e) | our |
| *pulcher* | *pulchra* | *pulchrum* | pulchr- (drops e) | beautiful |

You never have to guess: the feminine and neuter show you the base. English helps too, because "liberty" keeps the e and "pulchritude" doesn't. After the masculine nominative (and vocative) singular, everything is regular:

| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | noster | nostr**a** | nostr**um** |
| gen. sg. | nostr**ī** | nostr**ae** | nostr**ī** |
| dat. sg. | nostr**ō** | nostr**ae** | nostr**ō** |
| acc. sg. | nostr**um** | nostr**am** | nostr**um** |
| abl. sg. | nostr**ō** | nostr**ā** | nostr**ō** |

The plural is *nostrī, nostrae, nostra* and so on, exactly like *bonī, bonae, bona*. Wheelock's examples are *līber* and *pulcher*; his appendix of forms declines them in full.

- *Patria nostra est lībera.* "Our country is free."
- *Puellae pulchrae in agrō manēbant.* "The beautiful girls were staying in the field."

## Notes from the word list

- **Satis**, "enough," is **indeclinable**, like *nihil*: one form serves every case. It can be a noun, often with a genitive (*satis glōriae*, "enough glory," literally "enough of glory"); an adjective, "sufficient" (*Ōtium est satis.* "Leisure is enough."); or an adverb, "sufficiently" (*satis bonus*, "good enough").
- **Igitur**, "therefore," is **postpositive**: it normally stands after the first word or phrase of its sentence, and is rarely first. *Perīcula igitur superābimus.* "Therefore we will overcome the dangers."
- **Tē**, "you," is the accusative and the ablative singular, like *mē*. The other forms of these pronouns come in section 11.
- **-ne** turns a statement into a question when the speaker doesn't know the answer. Attach it to the first word, usually the verb or whatever word the question is about: *Manēbisne crās?* "Will you stay tomorrow?" Other kinds of question come much later.
- **Animus** means "mind" or "spirit," but the plural *animī* often means "courage" or "pride": *Nautae magnōs animōs habēbant.* "The sailors had great courage."
- **Quandō** asks or tells "when": *Quandō cēnābimus?* "When will we dine?" After *sī*, "if," it means "ever."
- **Maneō** and **remaneō** are listed together and mean the same thing, "remain, stay."
- **Culpa**, "fault, blame," and **culpō**, "to blame," are a matched pair: *Propter culpam tuam poenās dabimus.* "Because of your fault we will pay the penalty." **Propter** takes the accusative.
- **Tum** is "then," in time: *Tum puerī manēbant.* "Then the boys were staying."

## A reading tip

Choose the meaning that fits the scene. In a passage about soldiers, *animī* is "courage," not "minds"; and the imperfect's five shades are all on the table until the context rules some out.

For each verb, name its tense, person, number and conjugation before translating. Practice by conjugating each new verb in all three tenses of the present system, and by declining noun–adjective pairs such as *animus līber* and *caelum nostrum*.

## Check yourself

1. Translate *Glōriam patriae servābitis.*
   - Hint: find the tense sign between the stem and the ending.
   - Answer: "You (pl.) will preserve the glory of the country." The -bi- marks the future.
2. Give the imperfect of *maneō* in the 3rd person plural, and say why its a is short.
   - Hint: present stem, then -ba-, then the ending.
   - Answer: *manēbant*, "they were staying." A long vowel goes short before -nt.
3. Give two possible translations of *Puer caelum vidēbat.*
   - Hint: the imperfect can show an action in progress or a habit.
   - Answer: "The boy was looking at the sky" or "The boy used to see the sky" (also "kept looking at").
4. What is the base of *pulcher*, and how do you know?
   - Hint: look at the feminine.
   - Answer: *pulchr-*, because the feminine is *pulchra*.
5. Turn *Vocābis* into a yes/no question.
   - Hint: which suffix asks a question whose answer you don't know?
   - Answer: *Vocābisne?* "Will you call?"
