# Sectiō IX · Pointing words: hic, ille, iste

**The one idea to take from this section:** Latin has three words for pointing, sorted by distance: **hic** for what is near me, **iste** for what is near you, and **ille** for what is away from us both. Their odd forms are concentrated mostly in the genitive and dative singular, and nine common adjectives share exactly those two oddities.

English gets by with "this" and "that." Latin divides the space a little more finely, and these words show up in almost every sentence of real Latin, so they repay the effort of memorizing them now.

## Three ways to point

| Word | Points to | Example | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *hic* | something near the speaker | *hic ager* | this field (here, mine) |
| *iste* | something near the person addressed | *iste ager* | that field (by you, yours) |
| *ille* | something away from both | *ille ager* | that field (over there) |

Words like these are called **demonstratives**, from *dēmōnstrāre*, "to point out." They work as adjectives when they go with a noun (*haec porta*, "this gate") and as pronouns when they stand alone (*haec*, "this woman" or "these things"). Either way they agree with what they point to in gender, number, and case.

## The forms of *ille*

| Case | m. | f. | n. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | ill**e** | ill**a** | ill**ud** |
| gen. sg. | ill**īus** | ill**īus** | ill**īus** |
| dat. sg. | ill**ī** | ill**ī** | ill**ī** |
| acc. sg. | ill**um** | ill**am** | ill**ud** |
| abl. sg. | ill**ō** | ill**ā** | ill**ō** |
| nom. pl. | ill**ī** | ill**ae** | ill**a** |
| gen. pl. | ill**ōrum** | ill**ārum** | ill**ōrum** |
| dat. pl. | ill**īs** | ill**īs** | ill**īs** |
| acc. pl. | ill**ōs** | ill**ās** | ill**a** |
| abl. pl. | ill**īs** | ill**īs** | ill**īs** |

Compare it with *magnus, magna, magnum* from section 4. The whole plural is the same as *magnus*, and so are the accusative and ablative singular. Only four things are new:

- **nominative masculine** *ille*, ending in -e;
- **neuter** *illud* (nominative and accusative), ending in -ud;
- **genitive** *illīus*, one form for all three genders;
- **dative** *illī*, also one form for all three genders.

**Iste, ista, istud** works exactly like *ille*: *istīus*, *istī*, *istum*, and so on. Learn one and you have both.

## The forms of *hic*

| Case | m. | f. | n. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | **hic** | **haec** | **hoc** |
| gen. sg. | **huius** | **huius** | **huius** |
| dat. sg. | **huic** | **huic** | **huic** |
| acc. sg. | hunc | hanc | **hoc** |
| abl. sg. | hōc | hāc | hōc |
| nom. pl. | hī | hae | **haec** |
| gen. pl. | hōrum | hārum | hōrum |
| dat. pl. | hīs | hīs | hīs |
| acc. pl. | hōs | hās | **haec** |
| abl. pl. | hīs | hīs | hīs |

The bold forms are the ones to drill. Underneath, *hic* is a short word h- plus *magnus*-type endings, with an extra -c stuck on in many forms (*hun-c*, *hā-c*). That -c is what's left of an old pointing particle, -ce, roughly "here." Before that c an m becomes n, so *hunc* and *hanc* come from *hum-c* and *ham-c*; undo that and drop the -c, and *hunc*, *hanc*, *hōc*, *hāc* look like ordinary accusatives and ablatives; the plural has no -c at all except in the neuter *haec*.

Two traps:

- **Haec** is three different forms: feminine nominative singular ("this woman"), and neuter nominative and accusative plural ("these things"). Context decides.
- **Hoc** (nominative and accusative neuter) has a short o; **hōc** (ablative) has a long one.

In *huius* and *huic*, the *ui* is a diphthong, one gliding sound, as in "phooey." *Huius* is said "hui-yus": its i does double duty, finishing the diphthong of the first syllable and starting the second as a y sound.

These are the same paradigms Wheelock gives; there are no other models to learn.

## Saying them aloud

Recite across the rows, masculine, feminine, neuter, not down the columns: *ille, illa, illud*, then *illīus* three times, then *illī* three times, and on through the plural. Then do *hic* the same way. Said aloud, the rhythm sticks faster than a table does.

## As adjectives

As adjectives, demonstratives carry emphasis, so they usually come before their noun, the opposite of most Latin adjectives.

- *Haec porta antīqua est.* "This gate is old."
- *Hī puerī bonī sunt; illī nōn sunt.* "These boys are good; those aren't."
- *Huic puellae rosam dabō.* "I will give a rose to this girl."
- *Illīus poētae carmina laudāmus.* "We praise that poet's poems."
- *In hōc agrō manēbimus.* "We will stay in this field."
- *Ille morbus multōs necābat.* "That disease was killing many people."

## As pronouns

Standing alone, a demonstrative means "this person" or "that thing," and its gender and number tell you which. English usually needs a noun supplied: "man," "woman," "things."

- *Hic scrībit, illa cōgitat.* "This man is writing; that woman is thinking."
- *Haec nōn amō.* "I don't like these things."
- *Illī dē bellō cōgitābant.* "Those men were thinking about the war."

Latin rarely uses the neuter as a pronoun outside the nominative and accusative. For "of this thing" or "to that thing" it adds a noun such as *rēs*, "thing," which you'll meet later.

### Extra meanings

- **"The former," "the latter."** When two things have been mentioned, *ille* points back to the first and more distant one, *hic* to the second and nearer one. *Rēx et poēta ibi erant: ille pecūniam, hic glōriam amābat.* "The king and the poet were there: the former loved money, the latter glory."
- **"He, she, it."** Often a demonstrative is just a pronoun with very little pointing force. *Illa* may simply be "she."
- **"The famous."** *Ille* can mean "that well-known": *ille Cicerō*, "the famous Cicero."
- **"That ... of yours."** *Iste* points at the listener's side, and it often sounds scornful, like English "that precious ... of yours": *Istōs mōrēs nōn tolerābō.* "I won't put up with those habits of yours." It can also simply mean "such," of the kind you mention or have: *iste mōs*, "a custom like the one you describe."

## Nine adjectives with the same quirk

Nine everyday 1st/2nd-declension adjectives borrow the demonstratives' genitive and dative: **-īus** and **-ī** in the singular, for every gender. Everywhere else they behave exactly like *magnus* (*alius* has one more quirk, below).

| Case | m. | f. | n. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nom. sg. | tōt**us** | tōt**a** | tōt**um** |
| gen. sg. | tōt**īus** | tōt**īus** | tōt**īus** |
| dat. sg. | tōt**ī** | tōt**ī** | tōt**ī** |
| acc. sg. | tōt**um** | tōt**am** | tōt**um** |
| abl. sg. | tōt**ō** | tōt**ā** | tōt**ō** |
| nom. pl. | tōt**ī** | tōt**ae** | tōt**a** |

The rest of the plural continues like *magnus*: *tōtōrum*, *tōtīs*, and so on.

**Alius** has two further quirks. Its neuter is **aliud**, with the same -ud as *illud*. And for its genitive Romans usually borrowed **alterīus** from *alter*, instead of the regular *alīus*. Its dative is the regular *aliī*.

Wheelock's models are *sōlus* and *alius*; every word in the group follows the same pattern.

### UNUS NAUTA

The classic way to remember the nine is the phrase UNUS NAUTA, one letter per word:

| | Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| U | *ūnus, -a, -um* | one |
| N | *nūllus, -a, -um* | no, none |
| U | *ūllus, -a, -um* | any |
| S | *sōlus, -a, -um* | alone, only |
| N | *neuter, neutra, neutrum* | neither |
| A | *alius, alia, aliud* | other, another |
| U | *uter, utra, utrum* | which one of a pair? either |
| T | *tōtus, -a, -um* | whole |
| A | *alter, altera, alterum* | the other one of a pair |

The phrase itself is a reminder of something from section 2: *nauta* is 1st declension but masculine, so "one sailor" is *ūnus nauta*, not *ūna*. And notice that every word on the list is about how many: one, none, any, only, which of two, other, the whole.

**Neuter** and **uter** drop the e in their other forms (*neutra*, *utrīus*), like *noster*; **alter** keeps it (*altera*, *alterīus*), like *līber*.

### In sentences

- *Ūnus vir patriam servābit.* "One man will save the country."
- *Fīliō sōlī pecūniam dabit.* "She will give the money to her son alone."
- *Nūllī virō pecūniam dabō.* "I will give money to no man."
- *Glōria tōtīus populī magna erat.* "The glory of the whole people was great."
- *Sine ūllā culpā.* "Without any blame."
- *Neutra soror manet.* "Neither sister is staying."
- *Utrum librum amās?* "Which of the two books do you like?"
- *Alter frāter scrībit, alter cēnat.* "One brother is writing; the other is dining."
- *Aliī manent, aliī errant.* "Some are staying, others are wandering."

Notice the last two: *alter ... alter* is "the one ... the other" (of two), and *aliī ... aliī* is "some ... others."

## Notes from the word list

- **Locus**, "place," is masculine in the singular, but its plural splits by meaning: neuter **loca**, *locōrum*, "places, a region," and masculine **locī**, *locōrum*, "passages" in a book. *Loca antīqua vidēre amō.* "I love seeing ancient places."
- **Nimis** (also **nimium**) cuts both ways. It can mean "too, too much": *Nimis cōgitās.* "You think too much." With an adjective or adverb it often means "very, exceedingly," in a good sense: *Fēmina nimis pulchra erat.* "The woman was extremely beautiful." Let context choose.
- **In** works like *sub* (section 7). With the ablative it means "in, on," for where something is. With the accusative it means "into, onto, toward," for motion, and sometimes "against." *Puerī in agrō sunt.* "The boys are in the field." *Puerōs in agrum dūcēbat.* "He was leading the boys into the field." As a prefix it can become il-, ir-, or im-: "invade," "illuminate," "irrigate," "impose." Don't confuse it with the negative prefix in-, "not," found in "insane" and "illiberal."
- **Enim**, "for, in fact," is postpositive, like *igitur*: it normally stands second in its clause, never first. *Manēre nōn possum; perīculum enim est magnum.* "I can't stay; for the danger is great."
- **Sōlus** gives the idiom **nōn sōlum ... sed etiam**, "not only ... but also": *Nōn sōlum fōrmam sed etiam sapientiam laudāmus.* "We praise not only beauty but also wisdom."
- **Studium** is "eagerness, enthusiasm, study," the kind of thing you have *for* something, which goes in the genitive: *Magnum studium sapientiae habet.* "He has a great passion for wisdom." A related verb, *studēre*, comes later.
- **Morbus** is "disease"; English "morbid" comes from it.

### Where these words went

*Ille* became the word for "the" in the Romance languages: Spanish *el* and *la*, Italian *il* and *la*, French *le* and *la*. It also became their words for "he" and "she" (French *il* and *elle*). *Ūnus* became their "a, an" (*un*, *una*, *une*). Others in the list survive too: *tōtus* in Italian *tutto*, French *tout*; *sōlus* in *solo*, *seul*; *alter* in *altro*, *autre*. So when you read *ille*, you're looking at the grandfather of *el* and *le*. Latin itself has no word for "the" or "a"; *ille* and *ūnus* only took on those jobs much later.

## A reading tip: -ī and -īus

Two endings now do more work than before.

- **-ī** on *illī*, *istī*, *tōtī*, *ūnī* can be **dative singular** (any gender) or **nominative plural masculine**. *Illī puerō* is "to that boy"; *illī puerī* is "those boys." Look at the noun it goes with.
- **-īus** on *illīus*, *tōtīus*, *nūllīus* is always **genitive singular**. Don't mix it up with nominatives like *fīlius*, which have a short i and belong to the 2nd declension.

To practice, take a masculine, a feminine, and a neuter phrase and decline each aloud, singular and plural: *hic ager*, *illa fēmina*, *istud tempus*, then *nūllus amīcus* and *tōta vīta*.

1. Translate *illī rēgī*, and say why *illī* can't be plural here.
   - Hint: look at the case of *rēgī*.
   - Answer: "to that king." *Rēgī* is dative singular, so *illī* must be too.
2. Give the genitive singular of *tōta terra*.
   - Hint: each word keeps its own declension.
   - Answer: *tōtīus terrae*.
3. Translate *Aliī cēnant, aliī errant.*
   - Hint: what does *aliī ... aliī* mean?
   - Answer: "Some are dining, others are wandering."
4. Which demonstrative would you use for "that letter of yours"?
   - Hint: whose side is the letter on?
   - Answer: *iste*: *istae litterae* (*litterae* is plural in form).
5. Translate *Puerī in illum agrum errābant.*
   - Hint: what case follows *in* here, and what does that tell you?
   - Answer: "The boys were wandering into that field." *In* + accusative means motion into.
