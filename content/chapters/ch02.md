# Sectiō II · The noun carries its job

**The one idea to take from this section:** in Latin, a noun's ending tells you what it is doing in the sentence, so the words can come in almost any order.

In English, *The girl praises the poet* and *The poet praises the girl* mean opposite things; only word order tells them apart. In Latin, *Puella poētam laudat* and *Poētam puella laudat* mean the same basic thing; the order only shifts the emphasis. The -m on *poētam* marks the poet as the one being praised, wherever the word sits.

## Nouns

A noun names something. It can be **common** (*porta*, a gate) or **proper** (*Vergilius*, Vergil, a name you'll meet often), and **concrete**, something you can point to (*rosa*), or **abstract**, an idea (*īra*, anger). Latin doesn't care about these labels grammatically; what matters is the noun's **case**.

## The cases and their jobs

A **case** is a set of endings that marks a group of related jobs. Latin has six. Each has a main use you need now; later sections add more, so it's worth keeping your own running list. (Prepositions and *Ō* appear in the examples below; both are explained under "Small words that connect.")

| Case | Main job | English clue | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | the subject | none (comes before the verb in English) | ***Puella*** *rosās amat.* The girl loves roses. |
| genitive | links one noun to another; often possession | "of," or 's | *fāma* ***poētae***, the poet's fame |
| dative | the indirect object: who receives or benefits | "to," "for" | ***Poētae*** *rosam dō.* I give a rose to the poet. |
| accusative | the direct object | none (comes after the verb in English) | ***Patriam*** *servant.* They save their country. |
| ablative | the "how, where, when" case | "by," "with," "from," "in," "on," "at" | *sine* ***pecūniā***, without money |
| vocative | someone you are speaking to | name set off by commas | *Valē,* ***nauta***! Goodbye, sailor! |

A few more details for each case:

- **Nominative** also follows *est*, "is," and other linking verbs to rename the subject: *Philosophia est* ***vīta*** *mea*, "Philosophy is my life." This is called the **predicate nominative**, and section 4 returns to it.
- **Genitive** usually comes right after the noun it belongs to.
- **Accusative** is also used after certain prepositions, such as *ad*, "toward," *post*, "after," and *in* meaning "into," all in later sections.
- **Ablative** is the most varied case. Without a preposition, it often answers "by what means?": *Pecūniā patriam cōnservant*, "They preserve the country with money." Its other uses are means, agent ("by whom"), accompaniment, manner, place where, place from which, and time; later sections name each one as it appears. When there's no preposition, supply "by," "with," or "from" in English.
- **Vocative** sometimes comes with the interjection *Ō*: *Ō fortūna!* In this declension it looks just like the nominative. Section 3 shows the main place where it doesn't.

## Declining a noun

Listing a noun's case forms in order is called **declining** it, and a family of nouns sharing one set of endings is a **declension**. This section's nouns are all **1st declension**.

A dictionary gives a noun in two forms plus its gender: *rosa, rosae, f.* The second form, the genitive singular, is the important one:

- its ending, -ae, tells you the declension;
- cutting off that ending gives the **base**, *ros-*, to which every other ending attaches.

Here is *rosa magna*, "the large rose," with the adjective agreeing in every form:

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | ros**a** magn**a** | ros**ae** magn**ae** |
| genitive | ros**ae** magn**ae** | ros**ārum** magn**ārum** |
| dative | ros**ae** magn**ae** | ros**īs** magn**īs** |
| accusative | ros**am** magn**am** | ros**ās** magn**ās** |
| ablative | ros**ā** magn**ā** | ros**īs** magn**īs** |
| vocative | ros**a** magn**a** | ros**ae** magn**ae** |

Latin has no words for "a," "an," or "the." *Rosa* can be "a rose" or "the rose"; choose whichever English needs.

### When one ending could mean several things

Several forms repeat:

- **-ae** can be genitive singular, dative singular, nominative plural, or vocative plural;
- **-īs** can be dative or ablative plural;
- **-a** and **-ā** differ only in vowel length: nominative *rosa*, ablative *rosā*. This is one place where macrons pay off immediately.

The verb usually settles it, because **a verb agrees with its subject in person and number**:

- *Puellae rosās dant.* The plural verb needs a plural subject, so *puellae* is nominative plural: "The girls give roses."
- *Puellae rosās dat.* Now the verb is singular, so *puellae* can't be its subject. It could be dative or genitive; giving is done *to* someone, so dative fits best: "She gives roses to the girl."

When the verb doesn't settle it, the sense of the sentence does.

## Gender

Every Latin noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter. For people, gender usually follows the person: a girl is feminine. For everything else it is a grammatical label with no connection to meaning. Gates are feminine for no reason you could guess, so learn each noun's gender along with its meaning.

Most 1st-declension nouns are feminine. The main exceptions are words for men's roles in Roman society, which are masculine: *nauta*, sailor; *agricola*, farmer (in section 3's list); and *poēta*, poet.

## Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun; used simply as a description, as in *rosa magna*, "a large rose," it is called an **attributive** adjective. It must **agree** with that noun in **gender, number, and case**.

A dictionary gives an adjective in its three genders: *magnus, magna, magnum* (masculine, feminine, neuter). The feminine *magna* uses exactly the 1st-declension endings above. To find an adjective's base, drop the -a from the feminine: *magn-*. This matters for adjectives like *pulcher, pulchra*, "beautiful," where the masculine hides the base (*pulchr-*). This section uses the feminine forms; sections 3 and 4 add the masculine and neuter.

**Agreement means the same gender, number, and case, not identical endings.** *Nauta* is masculine, so "a great sailor" is *nauta magnus*, not *\*nauta magna*, even though *nauta* ends in -a. The masculine endings arrive in section 3.

Where the adjective goes:

- **usually right after its noun:** *patria antīqua*, the ancient homeland;
- **before it when it describes size or quantity:** *magna fāma*, great fame; *multae rosae*, many roses;
- **before it when the writer wants emphasis.**
- **before it if it's a demonstrative** ("this," "that"), which come later.

Poetry ignores these habits freely, which you'll see in Vergil.

## Small words that connect

**Prepositions** come before a noun, their object, and put it in a particular case, usually accusative or ablative; a few take either, with a difference in meaning. *Sine*, "without," takes the ablative: *sine pecūniā*. A few take either case with a change of meaning: *in* + ablative is "in, on," but *in* + accusative is "into."

A prepositional phrase can describe a noun, which makes it **adjectival** ("the girl without money"), or a verb, which makes it **adverbial** ("she lives without money").

**Conjunctions** join things:

- **coordinating** conjunctions join equals: *fāma et fortūna*, reputation and luck; *Puellam laudant sed poētam nōn laudant*, they praise the girl but not the poet.
- **subordinating** conjunctions attach a dependent clause to a main one, like *sī* from section 1: *Sī pecūniam amās, errās*, "If you love money, you're going astray."

**Interjections** are exclamations dropped into a sentence, like *Ō*.

## Describing what a word is doing

Grammar has two halves:

- **morphology**, the forms of words;
- **syntax**, how words fit together into sentences.

To describe a word's syntax, give three things:

1. its form;
2. the word it depends on;
3. its job.

Take *Poēta rosās puellīs dat*, "The poet gives roses to the girls."

| Word | Form | Depends on | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *poēta* | nominative singular | *dat* | subject |
| *rosās* | accusative plural | *dat* | direct object |
| *puellīs* | dative plural | *dat* | indirect object |

*Puellīs* could also be ablative plural. Dative fits here because roses are given *to* someone.

## Reading strategy, updated

1. Find the verb and note its person and number.
2. Look for a nominative that matches it: that's your subject. If there isn't one, the subject is inside the verb ("he," "she," "they").
3. An accusative is what the verb acts on; a dative is who receives it.
4. Fit the remaining pieces (genitives, ablatives, adjectives) to the nouns and verb they belong to.

## Check yourself

1. What is the base of *sententia, sententiae*?
   - Hint: start from the genitive.
   - Answer: *sententi-*.
2. Name both cases *fōrmīs* could be.
   - Hint: which ending did the declension table use twice in the plural?
   - Answer: dative or ablative plural.
3. Translate *Nautae patriam amant*.
   - Hint: let the verb's number decide what *nautae* is doing.
   - Answer: "The sailors love their country." (The plural verb makes *nautae* nominative plural.)
4. What's wrong with *\*poēta magna*?
   - Hint: check the gender of *poēta*.
   - Answer: *poēta* is masculine, so the adjective must be masculine: *poēta magnus*, using the masculine form you'll learn properly in section 3.
5. Translate *Sine pecūniā nōn valēmus*.
   - Hint: *sine* tells you the case of *pecūniā*; the verb tells you the subject.
   - Answer: "Without money we aren't strong."
