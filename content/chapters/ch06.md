# Sectiō VI · "Was," "will be," and "can"

**The one idea to take from this section:** the past and future of *sum* are regular once you know their stem, er-, and *possum*, "I can," is nothing more than *sum* with pot- in front.

The section also introduces the **complementary infinitive**, the infinitive that finishes the meaning of verbs like "can" and "ought."

## *Sum* in the future and imperfect

The present of *sum* (section 4) had to be memorized as six odd forms. The other two tenses of the present system are much tidier: both use the stem **er-** plus endings you already know.

| | Future | Imperfect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | er**ō** | er**am** |
| 2nd sg. | er**is** | er**ās** |
| 3rd sg. | er**it** | er**at** |
| 1st pl. | er**imus** | er**āmus** |
| 2nd pl. | er**itis** | er**ātis** |
| 3rd pl. | er**unt** | er**ant** |
| English | I will be, you will be . . . | I was, you were . . . |

Set these beside section 5 and you'll see the family likeness. The future runs -ō, -is, -it, -imus, -itis, -unt, like -bō, -bis, -bit . . . -bunt without the b. The imperfect runs -am, -ās, -at, -āmus, -ātis, -ant, like -bam, -bās, -bat . . . -bant without the b.

Just as *est* and *sunt* can mean "there is" and "there are," *erit* and *erat* can mean "there will be" and "there was":

- *Erunt semper bella.* "There will always be wars."
- *Erat tyrannus malus.* "There was a wicked tyrant." (Or "He was a wicked tyrant"; context decides.)
- *Heri erāmus in perīculō.* "Yesterday we were in danger."
- *Crās erō in agrō.* "Tomorrow I will be in the field."

## *Possum*, "I can"

*Possum, posse, potuī* means "to be able, can, could." It is a compound: pot- (from an old adjective *potis*, "able"; compare English "potent") joined to the forms of *sum*.

One sound change makes it look less obvious than it is. **When the next letter is s, the t of pot- turns into s**, so *pot-sum* became *possum*. Before a vowel the t stays: **pot**es, **pot**erō. The infinitive *posse* is shortened from an older *potesse*.

| | Present | Future | Imperfect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | possum | poterō | poteram |
| 2nd sg. | potes | poteris | poterās |
| 3rd sg. | potest | poterit | poterat |
| 1st pl. | possumus | poterimus | poterāmus |
| 2nd pl. | potestis | poteritis | poterātis |
| 3rd pl. | possunt | poterunt | poterant |
| English | I am able, I can | I will be able | I was able, I could |

If you can say *sum* in all three tenses, you can say *possum*: put pot- in front of each form and change the t to s wherever *sum* begins with s. (The perfect *potuī* is learned separately.)

## The complementary infinitive

"I can" is incomplete on its own; you have to say what you can *do*. Latin is the same. *Possum* is normally followed by an infinitive that completes its meaning, and that infinitive is called **complementary** (from "complement," something that completes; nothing to do with compliments).

- *Possumusne tyrannum superāre?* "Can we overcome the tyrant?"
- *Discipulī verba Graeca vidēre nōn poterant.* "The students couldn't see the Greek words."
- *Īnsidiās tolerāre nōn poterimus.* "We will not be able to put up with treachery."
- *Nunc remanēre nōn possum.* "I can't stay now."

You've already used this construction with *dēbeō*: *Dēbētis librōs vestrōs servāre.* "You ought to keep your books." A verb meaning "dare," in section 7, works the same way, and so do others later.

**A complementary infinitive has no subject of its own.** Whoever "can" or "ought" is also the one who does the infinitive's action. So if the infinitive is *esse* with a predicate adjective, that adjective agrees with the main subject: *Salvī esse possumus.* "We can be safe." *Salvī* is nominative plural because "we" is.

## Notes from the word list

- **Deus**, "god," is irregular in a few forms. The vocative singular is the same as the nominative, *deus*. In the plural, the short forms *dī* and *dīs* are standard; *deī* and *deīs* also became common from Augustus' time on.

  | Case | Singular | Plural |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | nominative | deus | dī (deī) |
  | genitive | deī | deōrum |
  | dative | deō | dīs (deīs) |
  | accusative | deum | deōs |
  | ablative | deō | dīs (deīs) |
  | vocative | deus | dī (deī) |

  **Dea**, "goddess," uses *deābus* for the dative and ablative plural, to keep it apart from the masculine, as *fīlia* does with *fīliābus*.
- **Īnsidiae**, "ambush, plot," has only plural forms, but the meaning is singular: *Īnsidiae erant malae* can mean "The plot was wicked."
- **Ibi**, "there," answers the question **ubi**, "where?" *Ubi est liber meus? Ibi est.* "Where is my book? It's there." To keep them straight, note that *ibi* comes before *ubi* in the alphabet, as "there" comes before "where."
- **Vester**, "your," is used when you speak to more than one person; **tuus** is for one. *Liber tuus* is "your book" said to one student; *librī vestrī* is "your books" said to a class. *Vester* declines like *noster*.
- **-que**, "and," is **enclitic**: it attaches to the end of the second of the two words it joins. *Dī deaeque* is "gods and goddesses," the same as *dī et deae*.
- **Liber** (short i), "book," a noun, and **līber** (long ī), "free," an adjective, differ only in the macron, which is one more reason to say every word aloud as you learn it. *Puer līber librum habet.* "The free boy has a book."
- **Graecus** is an adjective, "Greek," and also a noun, "a Greek": *Graecī multōs librōs habēbant.* "The Greeks had many books."
- **Quārē** is literally "because of which thing," but usually means "therefore" or "why": *Quārē nōn cēnās?* "Why aren't you dining?" *Perīculum est magnum; quārē in agrīs manēbimus.* "The danger is great; therefore we will stay in the fields."
- **Secundus** means "second," but also "favorable," since the "following" wind is the helpful one: *fortūna secunda*, "good fortune."

## Two things you'll see in real Latin

- Writers like to arrange paired phrases in a mirror pattern, A B B A, called **chiasmus**: *Amō patriam; patria mē amat.* "I love my country; my country loves me." The verbs *amō* and *amat* frame the two clauses, and *patriam patria* meet in the middle, which sharpens the contrast.
- Latin can make "little" or "dear" versions of nouns with suffixes such as -ulus/-ula and -olus/-ola: *amīculus*, "dear little friend"; *fīliola*, "little daughter." You'll meet them in the readings; the base word tells you the meaning.

2. Why is it *possum* but *poterō*?
   - Hint: what letter follows pot- in each?

   - Hint: *erit* is future.

   - Hint: the suffix goes on the second word.
